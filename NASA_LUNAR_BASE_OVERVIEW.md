# NASA Lunar Base: Plain-Language Overview

**Information snapshot:** 2026-08-22  
**Purpose:** A nontechnical summary of information useful for planning and explaining a future NASA lunar base.

## What the lunar base is intended to do

NASA's Moon Base effort is planned as a gradual build-up rather than a single construction mission. Robotic spacecraft will first deliver instruments, supplies, communications equipment, and mobility systems. Later crewed Artemis missions are expected to expand operations, use the delivered equipment, and establish a more regular human presence near the lunar South Pole.

The main goals are to:

- Learn how to live and work on the Moon for longer periods.
- Study water ice and other useful lunar resources.
- Test ways to move people, cargo, and science equipment across the surface.
- Establish dependable communications and navigation around the Moon.
- Develop experience that can support future missions to Mars.
- Build an international and commercial lunar exploration partnership.

## Why the lunar South Pole matters

The South Pole is the main focus because some areas may contain water ice, while nearby high ground may receive useful periods of sunlight. The region is also difficult to operate in: the Sun stays low on the horizon, shadows can last a long time, temperatures can change sharply, and some places cannot communicate directly with Earth.

For a future base, the most important local questions are:

- Where water ice and other resources are located.
- Which areas are safe for landing and driving.
- Which locations receive enough sunlight for power.
- How equipment and crews can communicate with Earth and with one another.
- How people and machines can survive long periods of darkness and cold.

## Planned build-up

### 1. Robotic preparation

Commercial and international robotic missions are expected to survey the surface, study resources, deliver equipment, and test operations before a permanent crew presence.

Important examples include:

- **Blue Moon Endurance:** a landing demonstration associated with NASA's Moon Base plans.
- **Griffin-1:** a planned South Pole delivery carrying the Astrolab FLIP rover and instruments for mobility, dust, mapping, and resource studies.
- **IM-3 Trinity:** a mission to study Reiner Gamma and begin testing lunar communications relay services.
- **Blue Ghost Mission 2:** a far-side mission carrying the Rashid Rover 2 and communications-related equipment.
- **IM-4:** a planned South Pole delivery focused on water and polar-environment investigations.
- **VIPER delivery:** a conditional plan to send NASA's rover to search for and map water ice.
- **MoonFall:** a planned group of flying vehicles for mapping, hazard inspection, and resource observations.

### 2. Surface mobility and cargo operations

NASA and its partners plan to deliver vehicles that can carry people, tools, and supplies across the lunar surface. These vehicles would support science, construction, maintenance, and travel between operational areas.

Named mobility systems include:

- **Astrolab FLIP:** an early logistics and mobility demonstration.
- **CLV-1:** a larger vehicle planned for crewed and autonomous cargo movement.
- **Pegasus:** another planned vehicle for crewed and cargo mobility.
- **VIPER and LUPEX rovers:** resource-focused vehicles for studying polar materials and possible water ice.

### 3. Crew and base operations

Artemis missions are intended to bring people back to the lunar surface and continue building the capabilities needed for a Moon Base.

- **Artemis III:** currently described as a systems demonstration in Earth orbit rather than a lunar landing.
- **Artemis IV:** currently targeted as the first Artemis lunar surface landing in the reviewed plan.
- **Artemis V:** intended to continue the lunar surface campaign and expand Moon Base capability.

The exact landing sites, schedules, vehicles, and crew activities may change as NASA and its partners revise the program.

## Important supporting services

A useful lunar base needs more than landers and habitats. It also needs shared services that allow people and machines to work safely.

### Communications and navigation

Relay satellites and planned lunar communications networks can connect the surface with Earth, especially in areas where Earth is not visible. Lunar Pathfinder, Altus-1, and ESA's Moonlight program are examples of planned communications or navigation support.

### Resource knowledge

Missions such as VIPER, LUPEX, Chang'e 7, Chang'e 8, MAGPIE, and LASSO are expected to improve knowledge of water, ice, soil, and other lunar resources. This information can help NASA choose useful locations and plan future deliveries.

### Power and survival

Base planning must account for sunlight, long shadows, extreme cold, dust, and the need to keep equipment operating during periods without direct sunlight. These environmental conditions affect where a base can be placed and how much equipment must be delivered.

### Transport and logistics

The base will depend on repeated deliveries of food, tools, spare parts, science instruments, power equipment, communications equipment, and mobility systems. NASA's planned commercial delivery model is intended to make these deliveries more frequent and flexible.

## International and commercial participation

NASA's lunar-base effort is expected to involve many partners. Commercial companies are developing landers, rovers, communications systems, and human-landing systems. International partners include Canada, Europe, Japan, India, the United Arab Emirates, and others.

Other major lunar programs, including China's Chang'e missions, may also contribute to broader knowledge of the lunar South Pole and resource use, although they are not NASA base missions.

## Locations relevant to NASA planning

- **Nobile Region:** a South Pole area associated with Griffin-1 and resource-focused operations.
- **Shackleton Connecting Ridge:** a South Pole ridge of interest for landing and sunlight studies.
- **The wider South Pole region:** the primary setting for future NASA base operations, mobility, resource surveys, and crewed missions.
- **Far-side regions:** scientifically valuable but dependent on relay communications.
- **Reiner Gamma:** a non-polar science location associated with IM-3 and lunar magnetic studies.

Specific landing coordinates and final base locations remain subject to future mission decisions.

## Useful planning categories for a lunar-base model

For a clear NASA lunar-base picture, organize information into these categories:

1. Missions and delivery dates.
2. Landing locations and areas of operation.
3. People, landers, rovers, drones, and cargo.
4. Power, communications, and navigation services.
5. Water, ice, soil, and other resources.
6. Surface travel and transportation routes.
7. Science, construction, maintenance, and daily operations.
8. Mission status, confidence, and unresolved decisions.
